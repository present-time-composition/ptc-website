Testimonies

Marcelo Moguilevsky

The dialogue between composition and improvisation, both in music and in any other art form, is a question Alan Bern has explored since the very beginning of his artistic journey, beyond musical genres, in search of a world of his own.

PTC offers us questions and also some answers, undoubtedly constituting a legacy for us, the musicians and artists who have the privilege of experiencing and enjoying it.

Throughout all the areas it encompasses, there is a profound reflection — I would even call it clinical — arising from the everyday practice of creation. A meticulous observation of the mechanisms of stimulus and response within the individual, both from within and in their relationship with the outside world, as well as of the dynamics that emerge within the social environment surrounding them.

With the commitment and love of someone devoted to teaching and the transmission of knowledge, Alan has created a system that does not seek to establish a truth, but rather to open up a connection. A connection that fosters deep listening through precise exercises and allows us to train our creativity, letting go of the mere repetition of structures in order to venture instead into creating them.

Perhaps one of PTC’s greatest contributions lies precisely here: it does not teach us what to create, but rather helps us discover and build the conditions for creation to happen. A space where listening, attention, interaction, and the present moment become the living substance of composition.

Shiau-uen Ding

My musical training as a pianist was rooted in European classical music. It gave me solid sight-reading skills, but not the ability to improvise. Nor did I have any idea where to begin learning how.

I participated in Alan’s original Real-Time Composition (now Present-Time Composition) class in Cincinnati. Through progressive stages of learning to sense and express impulses, listen and respond, and shape and build on others’ music in solo, duo, and large-ensemble settings, I gradually developed the confidence and skills to improvise in the language of contemporary music. This opened up an entirely new world of musical possibilities for me and tremendously expanded my repertoire as a pianist specializing in New Music.

The experience extended far beyond the classroom and Cincinnati. Our group continued to work and play together, performing in various cities. Along the way, we became best friends, actually a family.

Michael Ippolito

My time studying improvisation with Alan Bern remains one of the defining moments in my musical education. In my own creative practice as a composer and in my position as a teacher of composition, I constantly refer to concepts I learned from Alan and which I explored deeply with my fellow musicians in the Cincinnati Real-Time Composers. These concepts include reframing musical ideas, what it means to establish an axis of communication, how to observe your impulses, along with many other ideas. During this formative time, I learned how to really listen – deeply and generously, but also with discipline, as a skill that can be developed and improved.

Steve Whipple

Learning Present-Time Composition with Alan in college was foundational in my development as a musician. It’s been around 20 years since I was in that class, but I still feel that was some of the most meaningful music making I’ve done. I learned how to listen, interpret, and respond (which really are the fundamentals of ensemble playing) with such detail and creativity. And being in an ensemble that learned these fundamentals together created a culture of trust and openness in the ensemble, which made it feel like a safe space to make music freely and take chances. This lead to some music making that felt deeply meaningful.

The reality of being a professional musician is that it’s difficult to spend a significant amount of time discussing and exploring the fundamentals of ensemble playing in this way. I wish we all had this type of training so we had the shared vocabulary to discuss and improve music making in any context.

Cesar Lerner

Discovering the work of my colleague and mentor, Dr. Alan, was eye-opening.

As a child, my approach to music was through improvisation, and I built my musical identity on that foundation. However, recognizing, analyzing, systematizing, and giving prominence to that ‘spark’ or ‘primal act’ that is IMPULSE—and understanding its scope as a method of composition—with Alan’s guidance, not only consolidated my identity, but also became a fundamental tool for passing on my own processes.

Vivien Zeller

PTC changed how I see music, improvisation and teaching.

PTC focuses on the musical interaction between people rather than on technique and theory. It helped me to develop a new level of awareness and intensity in listening, feeling, playing, connecting and responding to others in a musical way.

Through PTC, I have learned to reliably access my own musical impulses and to use them in a deliberate, conscious manner. This results in a far more authentic, musically diverse, and responsive style of playing—both when performing with others and when playing solo.

I use aspects of PTC during my work as a violin teacher and during improvisation classes for musicians. There I can observe the immediate, tangible impact this approach has on how people play.
